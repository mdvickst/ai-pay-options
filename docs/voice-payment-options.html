Taking card payments in an AI voice agent call: four options on Twilio

A practical comparison of <Pay>, the agent-assisted Payments API, a dedicated ConversationRelay payment session, and <Gather>. It covers how each one works, which systems end up in PCI scope, how much you'll have to build, and what we learned building all four behind a working AI agent.

At a glance

Every option starts the same way. A caller is talking to an AI voice agent, the agent confirms the amount, and the caller agrees to pay. The agent can be built on ConversationRelay (as in our reference demo) or on bidirectional Media Streams with your own speech pipeline or a speech-to-speech model. What differs is which system collects the card details, which systems can see or hear them, and how the caller gets back to the agent afterwards.

What ends up in PCI scope

The main question for your security team is simple: which systems ever see a full card number, CVV or expiry date? Anything that does is in scope for PCI DSS. Options 1 and 2 keep card data inside Twilio and your payment processor. Options 3 and 4 send it through your own services.

Option 1

<Pay> verb

Smallest PCI scopeLow effort

When the caller agrees to pay, the agent says a short hand-off line and your app redirects the live call to TwiML containing <Pay>. Twilio prompts for each field, validates the input, sends it to the Pay Connector, and posts the result to your action URL. That URL returns TwiML that reconnects the caller to the agent, which opens with the outcome.

Your action URLGets the result and reconnects the agent
TwilioYour systemsHandles card data

Pros

  • Card data never reaches your app or the LLM, so this has the smallest PCI scope.
  • Twilio handles validation, retries (maxAttempts), timeouts and the connector call.
  • Each field's prompt can be customized for each error type, attempt number and card type with <Prompt>.
  • Very little code: one TwiML document, one status callback, one action handler.

Cons

  • The agent leaves the call, so the caller can't ask a question in the middle of entering their card.
  • Keypad only, with scripted prompts. It's less conversational than the rest of the call.
  • The <Pay> voice can differ from the agent's ConversationRelay voice unless you match them.
  • Reconnecting starts a new ConversationRelay session, so you have to carry the conversation context across yourself.

What we learned

  • Redirect when the hand-off line finishes playingRedirecting as soon as the agent starts its hand-off sentence cuts it off mid-word, and a fixed delay is either too short or leaves dead air. Subscribe to playback events with events="speaker-events tokens-played" on <ConversationRelay>. Once the hand-off text has been sent, redirect the call when ConversationRelay reports that the agent has stopped speaking. Keep a timeout as a fallback in case no event arrives.
  • Carry the outcome back to the agentPut the result in the agent's history and greeting, for example "Your payment of $80.38 was successful. Anything else?". The new session then picks up naturally.
Option 2

Agent-assisted Payments API

Smallest PCI scopeMedium–high effort

The call stays connected to the agent. Your app creates a Payment session on the live call (POST /Calls/{CallSid}/Payments), then sends one update per field (Capture=payment-card-number, then expiration-date, and so on). While Twilio is collecting a field, it intercepts the keypad tones, so neither the agent nor your app ever receives the digits. Status callbacks report masked progress after each keypress and when each field finishes. When every field is in, Status=complete sends the card to the connector.

AI agentHandles anything the caller says
↔

Twilio Payments APICaptures the keypad tones and sends masked callbacks
→
Pay ConnectorCharges or tokenizes the card
TwilioYour systemsHandles card data

Pros

  • The same small PCI scope as <Pay> (with call audio suppressed during capture), while the agent stays on the call.
  • The agent stays on the line between fields, so the caller can ask a question or say "I typed it wrong" and get an answer.
  • You have full control over the flow: field order, prompt wording in the agent's voice, retry rules, silence handling, and when to complete or cancel.
  • Masked progress for each keypress (for example "7 digits entered") lets you coach the caller.

Cons

  • You build the whole state machine: field order, prompts, retries, silence detection, completion and cancellation.
  • Twilio plays no prompts in this mode. Everything the caller hears comes from your app.
  • Callbacks arrive asynchronously, and many of them, so timing problems are easy to create.
  • Letting an LLM drive each step is slow and unpredictable (see below).
Call audio keeps flowing during capture by defaultThe Payments API stops keypad tones from reaching your app, but by default the call's audio still goes to your agent while Twilio is collecting a field. If the caller reads their card number aloud, or their phone sends keypad tones in the audio (in-band DTMF), those card details can reach your speech-to-text, transcripts, LLM, recordings or Media Streams. Ask your Twilio account team to set the account flag that stops audio transmission while digit collection is in progress. With it set, the agent can't hear the caller while Twilio is collecting a field, so design the conversation to handle questions between fields.
Don't let the LLM drive the capture sequenceOur first version turned every Payments API callback into an LLM turn. That caused stale prompts ("enter the expiration date" after the caller had already entered it), repeated sentences, fields asked for out of order, and a payment that processed before the zip code prompt played. A reasoning model can spend 5–20 seconds on one turn, so prompts arrive after the caller has moved on. The version that works keeps the capture sequence on the server and uses the LLM only when the caller speaks.

What we learned

  • Required uses capture namesThe callback's Required parameter lists hyphenated names such as expiration-date,security-code. Matching on names like ExpirationDate makes it look as if every field is done after the card number.
  • After an error you must request the field againTwilio doesn't retry on its own. Send Capture=<field> again with each re-prompt.
  • Don't send ChargeAmount and TokenType togetherCreating a session with both returned 64020 Invalid input in our testing. Send ChargeAmount to charge, or TokenType without an amount to tokenize.
  • Set PaymentConnector to the connector's unique nameUse the name shown in Console (for example Stripe_1), not its XB… SID. A SID returns 64004 in <Pay> and 64020 from the API.
  • The silence timer is yours to buildStart it after the prompt plays, stop it on the first keypress, and pause it while the caller is talking to the agent. A timer that fires while the caller is typing requests the field again and wipes their entry.
  • Run one agent turn at a time, and cancel it when the caller interruptsOverlapping turns overwrite each other's conversation history. Queue turns for each call, and cancel the current turn when the caller speaks over it, so a stale reply never plays.
  • Keep card details and confirmation codes out of the LLM promptGive the model only masked state ("waiting for the security code; 0 digits entered"). One reasoning model's safety classifier flagged a payment conversation as a policy violation. If that happens, retry with a minimal prompt and fall back to a fixed line.
  • Choose a low-latency model with thinking offThe LLM now only handles unscripted speech, so a small, fast model with reasoning disabled keeps those replies under about 2 seconds.
  • Suppress call audio during digit collectionBy default the Payment session takes the keypad tones but leaves call audio flowing, so ConversationRelay or Media Streams keep receiving the caller's voice and any in-band DTMF while Twilio is collecting a field. Have your account team set the flag that stops audio transmission during digit collection, then test that your agent stays silent and handles the caller's questions between fields.
Option 3

ConversationRelay payment session

Large PCI scopeHigh effort

After the hand-off line, your app redirects the call to a second <Connect><ConversationRelay> pointing at a separate WebSocket that is used only for payments. A deterministic parser (not an LLM) turns each transcript or keypress into digits, validates them, and reads back the brand and last four digits for confirmation. It then charges through your processor and ends the session with handoff data. The <Connect> action URL reconnects the caller to the agent.

Pros

  • The most natural experience: callers can say their card number or key it in, mixing the two freely.
  • Prompts can use the same voice, tone and speech settings as the agent.
  • Full control over parsing, confirmation, retries and wording.
  • The payment socket is separate from the agent, so the LLM never sees card data.

Cons

  • Your WebSocket server receives full card numbers, so it, its logs and its hosting are in PCI scope.
  • The speech-to-text and text-to-speech providers must be PCI-compliant ones. Conversation Intelligence (classic) must not be enabled.
  • You build the processor integration yourself; there's no Pay Connector.
  • Speech recognition errors on 16-digit numbers are common, so you need a confirmation step and careful normalization.

What we learned

  • Clean up the transcript before parsing itTranscripts arrive as 4111-1111…, 4O11, four one one one, double four or oh. Strip punctuation, remove spaces between digit groups, and convert number words. Inside tokens that already contain a digit, convert lookalike letters (O/Q→0, I/l→1, Z→2, S→5, B→8). Leave ordinary words alone.
  • Validate as you goRun a Luhn check and a brand-specific length check (Amex is 15 digits, most others 16) on the card number, and use the brand to choose a 3- or 4-digit CVV. Wait a few seconds after the last fragment before evaluating, because callers read numbers in chunks.
  • Confirm before chargingRead back "a Visa ending in 4 2 4 2", spacing the digits so text-to-speech reads them one at a time. Accept yes/no by voice or 1/2 by keypad.
  • Mask everything you logReplace digits and number words in any transcript you log or publish. Keep WebSocket frame logging off (or behind its own flag) anywhere card data flows.
  • Pause call recording during collectionIf the call is recorded, card details spoken or keyed during the payment session end up in the recording. Pause the recording with the Recordings API (Status=paused, with PauseBehavior=skip or silence) before the first payment prompt, and resume it (Status=in-progress) after the session ends.
  • Return through handoff dataEnd the payment socket with an end message carrying handoffData (the outcome only, never card data). The action URL uses it to greet the caller back in the agent session.
Option 4

<Gather> loop

Large PCI scopeMedium effort

The call is redirected to a <Gather input="speech dtmf"> for each field: card number, expiry, CVV, then zip code. Each <Gather> posts Digits or SpeechResult to your action webhook. The webhook normalizes and validates the input, then returns the next <Gather> or a re-prompt. After the last field, your code charges the card and reconnects the caller to the agent.

Pros

  • Speech and keypad entry with plain TwiML and webhooks; no WebSocket server.
  • Stateless, request/response handlers that are easy to reason about and test.
  • Built-in speech hints such as $OOV_CLASS_DIGIT_SEQUENCE, $MONTH $YEAR and $POSTALCODE.
  • <Say> supports ElevenLabs voices, so prompts can sound like the agent.

Cons

  • Your webhook receives raw card data, so it's in PCI scope, along with its logs.
  • Turn-by-turn and less fluid than ConversationRelay; the caller can't easily interrupt or ask questions.
  • You build the processor integration and the retry logic yourself.
  • Speech accuracy on long digit strings needs the same normalization as option 3.

What we learned

  • Give callers time to pauseWith speechTimeout="auto", recognition ended while callers paused between digit groups. A fixed speechTimeout="5" lets them read the number in chunks.
  • Set numDigits once the length is knownAfter the brand is known, set numDigits for expiry (4), CVV (3, or 4 for Amex) and zip (5) so keypad entry finishes on its own. Leave it unset for the card number, and finish with #.
  • Use HTTPS with a trusted certificateIn a PCI workflow, a <Gather> action URL over plain HTTP or with a self-signed certificate fails with error 13337.
  • Match the agent's voiceUse <Say voice="ElevenLabs.{voiceId}"> with the agent's voice ID so the caller doesn't hear a different voice during payment.
  • Pause call recording during collectionIf the call is recorded, card details spoken or keyed during the <Gather> steps end up in the recording. Pause the recording with the Recordings API (Status=paused, with PauseBehavior=skip or silence) before the first payment prompt, and resume it (Status=in-progress) after the last field.
  • Turn on actionOnEmptyResultYour webhook then sees no-input attempts and can count retries, instead of TwiML silently moving on.

Applies to all four options

These issues came up regardless of which option we were building.

  • Turn on PCI Mode in a dedicated account or subaccount. It's set per account and can't be turned off. Once it's on, recordings are kept for one year and native or Marketplace transcription is unavailable.
  • Keep every Twilio resource in the same account. The API key, TwiML App, phone number and Pay Connector must belong to the account that owns the call. A TwiML App in another account gave us generic 31000 errors in the browser.
  • Never give the LLM card data. Pass it only results and masked state, and filter the model's output so it can't speak invented "system event" text to the caller.
  • Speak fixed lines for results. Don't read raw confirmation codes or error codes aloud. Map failures to plain reasons such as "your card was declined" or "the card number wasn't valid".
  • Mask before you log. Show only the last four digits of the card number and fully mask CVV and expiry. Give each kind of log (LLM, WebSocket, connector) its own on/off switch, and keep sensitive ones off in production.
  • If you host the Pay Connector endpoint, answer requests idempotently. Twilio can retry a request. Key your response on transaction_id and return the same charge_id or token_id every time.
  • Stop all work when the call ends. On the completed status callback, cancel timers and queued turns. Late updates to a finished call fail with 21220.
  • Pick a voice model that supports your formatting. Expressive tags like [warmly] only work on ElevenLabs v3/v4 voices. Other models may read them out loud.
  • Log the LLM prompts in development. Logging the full prompt and response is the quickest way to see why the agent did something unexpected.
  • Test the unhappy paths on real calls. Wrong digit counts, typing during a prompt, talking during entry, long silences, declines and hangups each produced different bugs in our testing.

Which option to choose

Start with <Pay> if…

you want the smallest PCI scope and the fastest launch, and a short scripted keypad step in the middle of an AI conversation is acceptable. It's the right default for most self-service payment lines.

Choose the Payments API if…

the agent must stay on the line during entry, to answer questions or coach the caller, and you still want card data kept out of your systems. Ask your account team to suppress call audio during digit collection, and budget for a server-side state machine that runs the capture sequence, and use the LLM only for unscripted speech.

Choose a ConversationRelay payment session if…

spoken card entry is a requirement, you already run a PCI-compliant environment, and you want full control over the experience in the agent's voice.

Choose <Gather> if…

you need speech entry but would rather build stateless webhooks than run a WebSocket service, and your webhook can live inside your cardholder data environment.

A common combinationUse the Payments API, with call audio suppressed during capture, as the default so card data stays out of your systems and the agent stays on the call. Fall back to <Pay> for callers who stall repeatedly. Add speech entry only if keypad entry measurably hurts completion rates.
